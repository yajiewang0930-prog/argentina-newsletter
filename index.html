📈 Macro & Policy

September inflation accelerated to 1.9% — still the lowest for that month since 2017 — while the IMF cut Argentina's growth forecast and flagged the fading disinflation tailwind. The first-half fiscal miss pushed the government toward a formal waiver.

  • September Inflation Accelerates to 1.9% as Food and Utilities Climb

    high

    INDEC reported September inflation of 1.9% month-over-month on October 13, accelerating from 1.7% in August but still the lowest September since 2017. Year-over-year inflation eased for a second straight month to 33.4%, with the January–September cumulative figure at 23.6%. Core inflation slipped to 1.7% from 1.8%, but the headline was pushed up by housing and utilities (+3.1%), education (+3.0%) and food and beverages (+2.0%), which carry the heaviest weight in the basket. Economy minister Luis Caputo highlighted the print as proof the anchor is holding, but analysts note the mix is shifting: regulated and seasonal prices are now driving the number even as core cools. Consultancies expect October to come in near 1.8–1.9%, leaving the full-year figure around 30%.

  • IMF Slashes 2026 Growth Forecast to 2.1% from 3.5%

    high

    The IMF's October World Economic Outlook cut Argentina's 2026 growth projection to 2.1%, down from 3.5%, citing weaker-than-expected activity through the third quarter, softer domestic demand and still-restrictive financial conditions. The downgrade lands as local consultancies warn the economy may have entered a technical recession, with August EMAE activity data pointing to stagnation. The Fund projects a recovery to 3.5% growth in 2027 and average inflation of 21.2%. The revision complicates the government's narrative that stabilization is already translating into durable expansion, and it raises the political stakes of the 2027 budget. For now, the growth shortfall is being absorbed by the program's external anchor — reserves remain above target — rather than by a loosening of fiscal or monetary policy.

  • Caputo Wins IMF Praise; H1 Fiscal Miss Forces a Waiver

    high

    Economy minister Luis Caputo met IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva in Bangkok on October 13, during the Fund's annual meetings, where she praised Argentina's fiscal discipline as "exemplary in the world." The warmth, however, masks an unresolved technical point: the government closed the first half with a primary surplus of $6.29 trillion, below the $6.86 trillion target — a roughly $570 billion shortfall even after the IMF had already relaxed the original requirement. Closing the third review will therefore require a formal waiver. Presidential officials insist Argentina "over-delivered" on other targets, notably reserves, and expect the waiver to be granted. The meeting confirmed both teams are working to finalize the review, which would unlock a US$860–869 million disbursement.

  • Disinflation Stalls on Services Inertia as FX Anchor Fades

    medium

    Even with the dollar stable, inflation has stopped falling, and analysts are increasingly worried about a fresh recession. A late-October assessment notes that services-price inertia is now the main obstacle: while core goods disinflation continues, services remain sticky, and the exchange-rate anchor that drove much of the earlier disinflation is losing force as the peso trades closer to the band ceiling. Real short-term interest rates have turned negative, and analysts warn the combination of stalled disinflation and a weakening anchor could rekindle devaluation expectations. The concern is that if the anchor slips, the hard-won 30% inflation floor could reverse — exactly the scenario the government's "Fase 2" tightening was meant to prevent. October's print will be the first test.

🏦 Financial & Credit

Household delinquency set another record and banks turned defensive, cutting consumer lending and pushing refinancings. Short real rates slipped negative, reviving dollarization fears, even as the BCRA kept buying dollars.

  • Household Delinquency Sets Record at 12.9%; 3.4M Over a Year Late

    high

    Bank delinquency among households reached 12.9% in July — the highest in more than two decades — up from 5.6% a year earlier, according to Fundación Pensar's "Pensar Argentina" report. Personal loans lead the deterioration at 16.7%, followed by credit cards at 12.8% and asset-backed loans at 8.1%; mortgages stay clean at 1.6%. Some 3.4 million debtors are now in the worst category, over a year overdue, up roughly 60% year-over-year, and about 5.7–5.8 million people carry some arrears. A Casa Tres survey shows 54% of respondents missed a payment recently, with 26% using credit mainly for food. The BCRA notes provisions cover 87.6% of the irregular portfolio and capital stands at 30.5% of risk-weighted assets — pressure on profits, not a systemic crisis.

  • Short Real Rates Turn Negative, Reviving Dollarization Risk

    high

    With monthly inflation around 1.9%, short-term peso rates have fallen below inflation, leaving real returns negative and reviving warnings about dollarization. One-day caución rates slid to roughly 19–23% TNA during October, while the TAMAR held near 23.75% — both below the 30% inflation rate. Analysts at Cohen and La Política Online argue the gap is pushing savers back toward dollars, exactly as the blue and CCL rates grind higher. The BCRA has so far resisted hiking the reference rate, preferring "Fase 2" liquidity-draining dollar sales. But the negative real-rate backdrop is a delicate position for a program built on peso demand; a renewed dollarization wave would test both reserves and the crawling-band anchor heading into the final quarter.

  • Banks Go Defensive: Consumer Credit Contracts, Refinancings Spread

    medium

    Argentine banks are retrenching as delinquency climbs. Consumer credit contracted again in September, according to Equilibra, with lenders tightening standards and pushing refinancing plans to clean up balance sheets. Real disposable income remains about 16% below 2023 levels, and the debt-service burden — roughly 24.1% of the wage bill — is forcing households to roll over obligations rather than take new loans. The stress is uneven: fintech-heavy and regional lenders show the sharpest deterioration, with specialists flagging particular pressure at Carrefour's BSF, Banco Columbia (where unrecoverable loans jumped 280%) and Tarjetas del Mar. Congress is debating more than 50 over-indebtedness bills, while the executive continues to treat household debt as a private matter. The net effect is a credit cycle that is shrinking precisely when the economy needs it to grow.

    Gross international reserves hovered around US$48.7 billion in early October, and the central bank kept accumulating — buying roughly US$915 million through mid-October, an average of about US$92 million a day — sustaining the program's strongest pillar.   • Brubank Up for Sale — Inter Leads Nubank and BTG Pactual

    high

    Brubank, one of Argentina's pioneering digital banks, is up for sale, and three Brazilian heavyweights — Nubank, BTG Pactual and Inter — are reportedly competing for the entity, with Inter seen as the frontrunner. The prize is Brubank's banking license and roughly five million users, assets that have become more valuable as the BCRA restricts fintechs' ability to hold salary accounts, USD CVUs and full financial intermediation. The sale reflects a broader consolidation: Cocos and Revolut already hold banking licenses, and Mercado Pago is expected to receive one before year-end, raising the price of the remaining independent digital banks. For Nubank, acquiring Brubank would offer a fast lane into Argentina's formal banking system after its earlier Nu Global launch.

  • Buenos Aires Province Opens Formal Case Against Six Fintechs

    high

    Buenos Aires Province formalized its investigation into Mercado Pago, Naranja X, Ualá, Personal Pay, Brubank and Cencosud in late October, after logging 2,240 consumer complaints in the first half of 2026. Governor Axel Kicillof and production minister Augusto Costa accuse the platforms of harassment and abuse in debt collection, unclear loan terms, opaque cost composition that can double or triple the advertised rate, and a missing "repentance" button. The firms must now present formal defenses; fines could reach $1,883 million plus reversal of abusive clauses. Kicillof frames the probe around record household debt — 60% of adults (about 21 million people) and some 6 million in default — blaming deregulation under DNU 70. The province's reach is limited, however, since rate-setting remains a national competence.
