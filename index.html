Caputo and Milei Rule Out Bailout for 5.8 Million Indebted Families as NPLs Hit 24-Year High

Argentina's household delinquency crisis reached a breaking point — and the government drew a hard line. With 5.8 million people in default (NPL ratio 12.8%) and 3.4 trillion pesos classified as irrecoverable, both President Milei and Economy Minister Caputo rejected any state assistance for over-indebted families. "Nobody put a gun to their head," Milei said, blaming banks for lending at 10% monthly while wages grew at 2%. Caputo added: "Each person must take responsibility for their decisions." Public banks continued refinancing — Banco Nación processed 62,000 cases ($310 billion) and Banco Provincia 67,000 ($270 billion) — but private banks tightened standards significantly. The political bet is clear: absorb the credit cycle without fiscal cost, and trust that disinflation will organically restore repayment capacity.

📈 Macro & Policy

July tested Argentina's disinflation narrative. After three consecutive monthly declines culminating in June's 1.9% — the first sub-2% print under Milei — inflation likely stalled or even ticked up to 2.0–2.1%, driven by winter holidays, World Cup tourism, and regulated tariff adjustments. Caputo successfully contained dollar pressure without reserve drawdowns, and the government advanced its BCRA charter reform to Congress. IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva visited Buenos Aires and endorsed the program's direction.

  • BCRA Charter Reform Sent to Congress — Five Pillars, But FX Controls Left Open

    medium

    The Executive submitted its BCRA Organic Charter reform to Congress with five pillars: a single mandate of preserving currency value; constitutional prohibition of monetary financing to the Treasury; a two-thirds majority requirement in both chambers to remove BCRA leadership; restrictions on profit transfers to the Treasury; and elimination of non-transferable Treasury bills held on the BCRA's balance sheet. The bill deliberately omits any provision on exchange controls — it neither prohibits nor hinders future imposition of capital controls, leaving the door open for a post-2027 administration. Critics, including former minister Domingo Cavallo, describe the reform as a "paper lock": since it is ordinary legislation rather than constitutional, a future government could reverse it with a simple law or emergency decree.

Caputo's rejection of government assistance — "each person must take responsibility" — marks a defining policy stance: the administration is betting that organic wage recovery and disinflation will restore repayment capacity without fiscal intervention.   • Credit Card Financing CFT Reaches 500% as Real Wages Remain Depressed

    medium

    The effective cost of consumer credit in Argentina has reached punitive levels. While headline personal loan rates are quoted at 65–90% TNA, the all-in CFT — including VAT on interest, provincial Ingresos Brutos turnover taxes, stamp duties, and mandatory insurance — pushes the real cost to 120–150% annually. Credit card revolving financing, when penalty rates are triggered after missed payments, can reach an effective annual rate of 500%. This creates a debt trap: a borrower who misses one payment sees their balance compound at multiples of wage growth (which remains near 2% monthly). The Mendoza Post reported that manufacturing output contracted 5.7% year-on-year in May, and real disposable income remains 14.5% below January 2023 levels — despite headline GDP growth of 0.7% in Q1.

  • Misiones Eliminates Provincial Tax on Digital Wallet Transfers for 200K Residents

    medium

    Misiones became the first Argentine province to eliminate Ingresos Brutos withholding on digital wallet and bank transfers, effective August 1. The measure covers monotributistas in categories A through D and individuals with monthly deposits up to ARS 2,184,404 — approximately 200,000 residents. The policy removes automatic deductions on both CBU (bank) and CVU (digital wallet) transfers, distinguishing personal use from commercial activity. Users of Mercado Pago, Modo, and Tarjeta Naranja had previously faced surcharges of 5–10% on electronic payments due to automatic tax withholding. The move directly addresses a key fintech industry demand: eliminating the tax distortion between traditional banking (where Ingresos Brutos is deducted at source) and digital payments. The Cámara Argentina Fintech has long argued that provincial turnover taxes are the single largest regulatory barrier to digital financial inclusion.
