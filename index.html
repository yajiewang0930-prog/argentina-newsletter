📰 Top Story

August Inflation Falls to 1.7% — a 14-Month Low — but Activity Stalls

INDEC reported August inflation of 1.7% month-over-month, the lowest in 14 months and the best August reading since 2017, down from 2.1% in July. Annual inflation eased to 33.5% and the eight-month accumulated figure reached 21.3%. Core inflation held at 1.8%, seasonal prices fell 0.9% — reversing July's 4.5% spike — and clothing actually declined 0.6% nominally. Milei, Caputo and Sturzenegger celebrated the print as proof the fiscal and monetary anchors are working. Markets responded with calm: sovereign risk fell to 482 basis points and the blue-dollar spread compressed to 1.85%, a two-month low. But the win is double-edged — manufacturing contracted 4.9% year-over-year in July, and Argentina has shed roughly 31,000 firms since December 2023, leaving disinflation's durability tied to a fragile real economy.

📈 Macro & Policy

August's 1.7% inflation — a 14-month low — restored the government's disinflation narrative and eased markets. The BCRA formalized a new 20–25% interest-rate corridor, while the charter reform advanced in Congress. But the real economy stalled: manufacturing and construction contracted, and Argentina shed 31,000 firms since December 2023.

  • August Inflation Drops to 1.7%, a 14-Month Low

    high

    INDEC confirmed August CPI rose just 1.7% month-over-month — the lowest in 14 months and the best August since 2017 — down from 2.1% in July. Annual inflation eased to 33.5%, with the eight-month accumulated figure at 21.3%. Consultancies expect a mild reacceleration in September: the BCRA's REM projects 1.8%, Eco Go 1.9%, and BBVA 1.6%. The key watch item is food — LCG measured a 3% jump in the second week of September, led by bread (+9.2%) and vegetables (+6.2%), which could test the trend.

  • BCRA Launches 20–25% Interest-Rate Corridor

    high

    The BCRA activated a new monetary-policy framework in early September: an interest-rate corridor with a 20% floor and a 25% ceiling. The central bank absorbs liquidity through passive repos (pases pasivos) at 20% and lends to banks via active repos at 25%, limited to a half-hour daily window. With no rigid reference rate, short-term Lecaps now signal pricing across the system. The BCRA also flexibilized reserve requirements, cutting the minimum daily integration from 75% to 65% and widening eligible assets. The move formalizes the shift away from a single policy rate toward a quantity-based regime. After August's FX tension pushed one-day repo rates to 28.2%, money-market rates retreated to around 19–20% by mid-September.

  • Activity Stalls — Manufacturing Down 4.9%, 31,000 Firms Lost

    high

    The inflation victory masks a stalling real economy. Manufacturing output fell 4.9% year-over-year in July (and 5% month-over-month), while construction contracted 4.5% year-over-year. Fundar calculates Argentina has lost roughly 31,000 companies since December 2023 — about 33 per day — leaving 481,724 active firms, the lowest in nearly two decades. Formal employment shed 411,613 jobs through May 2026. The peso's role as a nominal anchor is stark: the dollar has risen just 3.5% this year against 21% inflation, squeezing competitiveness. Analysts increasingly describe a "fine-tuning" dilemma — the government is succeeding on disinflation but is now under pressure to restart activity without loosening its fiscal or monetary anchors.

    medium

    The government's BCRA Charter reform won half-sanction in the Chamber of Deputies, advancing a bill that would limit the central bank to preserving the value of the currency and strip it of tools to steer credit and interest rates. Critics warn the move curtails the BCRA's capacity to respond to financial stress precisely as household delinquency climbs. The bill now moves to the Senate. Its passage, alongside the 2027 budget and a draft tax reform due in December, forms the institutional agenda the IMF is watching ahead of the third program review.

🏦 Financial & Credit

Delinquency kept climbing even as inflation fell, pushing banks and wallets to retrench. The IMF downplayed the risk, citing low household leverage and deep provisions. Argentina now holds the region's highest household default rate, and the BCRA opened direct-debit rails to ease fintech collections.

  • Personal-Loan NPL Tops 16% as Banks and Wallets Retrench

    high

    Delinquency keeps climbing even as inflation falls. Total credit irregularity reached 7.7% of loans, but the household segment is far worse: credit-card default exceeds 12% and personal-loan default tops 16% — the highest in 15 years. Some 5.8 million people carry some degree of arrears, 2.4 million of them with fintech or digital-wallet lenders. Banks and wallets have responded by retrenching: consumer and card lending fell 1.3% in real terms in August, personal loans dropped 7.6% year-over-year, and credit cards 10.1%. Lenders tightened repayment-capacity checks and cut limits, while offering refinancings — though nearly one in two borrowers who regularize falls back into default within a year.

  • IMF Downplays Delinquency as Systemic Risk, Urges More Credit

    high

    IMF spokesperson Julie Kozack said the Fund is monitoring Argentina's rising household delinquency but does not consider it a significant financial-stability risk, citing household debt of only about 8% of GDP — low by regional standards — and bank provisions covering more than 85% of non-performing loans. Banks remain well capitalized and liquid, she added. The Fund instead urged Argentina to deepen its "quite reduced" banking system and expand longer-term financing. The assessment comes as the IMF prepares a technical mission (September 21–28) for the third review of the Extended Fund Facility. Kozack also highlighted two consecutive years of primary fiscal surplus, inflation near 30%, resumed growth, and lower poverty as signs of "notable advances in stabilization."

  • Argentina Posts Region's Highest Household Delinquency

    medium

    Argentina now has the highest household delinquency rate in Latin America, exceeding Brazil (5.2%), Colombia (5.1%), Mexico (3.0%), Chile (2.6%) and Paraguay (2.3%). The family default rate climbed from 2.5% in October 2024 to around 12.8% by 2026, rising for sixteen straight months. The deposit-to-lending rate gap is also among the region's widest: fixed-term deposits average near 20–22% TNA while personal loans run near 65%, a spread of roughly 43 percentage points — almost double the 22.6-point historical norm. The combination of record default and expensive credit explains why consumption lending is shrinking rather than expanding, even as the government seeks to revive activity through credit.

  • BCRA Launches Direct-Debit Rails for Fintech Loan Collections

    medium

    The BCRA enabled the Cobro con Transferencia (CCT) scheme, effective August 31, letting fintechs such as Mercado Pago, Ualá and Naranja X debit loan installments directly from a borrower's bank account — savings, salary or checking — without manual transfers. The rules require express user consent, 24-hour advance notice, immediate revocation rights, a maximum of three attempts, and a cap of 30% of declared income. The mechanism is designed to reduce collection friction and, officials hope, lower default by making payments automatic. It also deepens interoperability between bank CBUs and wallet CVUs, which became fully cross-compatible in 2026. The move marks another step in formalizing digital lending as delinquency with wallets approaches 30%.

  • Kicillof Opens Probe Into Six Fintechs; Fines Up to $1,883M

    high

    Buenos Aires Province governor Axel Kicillof opened an investigation into six digital-lending platforms — Mercado Pago, Naranja Digital (Naranja X), Ualá, Personal Pay, Brubank and Cencosud — after the consumer-protection directorate logged 2,240 complaints in the first half of 2026. Each firm could face fines up to $1,883 million plus orders to reverse disputed practices, with imputations beginning around September 11. The charges allege opaque cost disclosure, annual charges exceeding 1,000% once penalties stack, algorithmic pricing that treats similar borrowers differently, aggressive collection calls, and a missing "repentance" button. Kicillof likened the situation to 2008 subprime lending, arguing wallets now act as financial entities without macroprudential oversight.

  • Wallet Delinquency Hits 30.1% as Credit Boom Sours

    high

    Digital-wallet default reached 30.1% in June 2026, up from 7.3% in November 2024 and above the pandemic peak, approaching 2001-crisis levels, according to think tank CEPA. Kicillof's government blames Milei's DNU 70/2023 — which scrapped lending rate caps — for fueling the boom, and says 60% of adults now carry debt while about six million people are in arrears. The data frames a growing political battle over jurisdiction: the province regulates from a consumer-protection angle while macroprudential power rests with the BCRA. The default is concentrated among younger, lower-income borrowers who took thin-file credit at the highest rates.

  • Top Fintechs Converge Toward Full Banking

    medium

    Argentina's top fintechs are converging toward full banking. Naranja X completed its migration from a CVU-based wallet to a BCRA-licensed digital bank with CBU, adding peso and dollar savings accounts, a Mastercard and fixed-term deposits. Ualá — already a bank since acquiring Wilobank — added USDT custody directly in its app this year, alongside its Ualá Bis merchant-acquiring arm. Mercado Pago remains a wallet with a CVU, but full CVU–CBU interoperability arrived in 2026, eroding a key advantage of bank accounts. On yields, Ualá pays 20.08% via its money-market fund, Naranja X 19%, and Mercado Pago 18.62%. The convergence raises the stakes in the regulatory fight, as platforms increasingly resemble banks without identical prudential rules.
