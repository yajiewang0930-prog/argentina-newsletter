July Inflation Rebounds to 2.1% as Markets Punish Argentine Assets

Argentina's disinflation streak snapped. INDEC confirmed July CPI rose 2.1% month-over-month, up from June's 1.9% and interrupting three straight months of declines, with annual inflation at 33.8%. The rebound was driven by seasonal winter-holiday effects — recreation jumped 5.0% and hotels 2.8% — plus a 7.4% surge in fresh vegetables and the lagged pass-through of the peso's ~6% depreciation. Markets reacted swiftly: sovereign risk spiked to 480 basis points (up 12% in August), the Merval shed 4.5% in dollar terms over five sessions, and the blue dollar widened its spread over the official rate to 3.8%. The BCRA moved to defend the inflation fight, slashing its dollar purchases to the lowest pace of 2026. Analysts call it a "pause," not a reversal — the REM projects 1.8% for August — but the episode exposed how fragile the disinflation narrative remains.

📈 Macro & Policy

July's 2.1% inflation print broke a three-month disinflation streak and rattled markets. The BCRA responded by slashing its dollar purchases to the lowest level of 2026 and formally consolidated a monetary-aggregates framework in its Q2 policy report.   • July Inflation Rebounds to 2.1%, Breaking Three-Month Disinflation Streak

    high

    INDEC confirmed July CPI rose 2.1% month-over-month, up from 1.9% in June and interrupting three straight months of disinflation. Annual inflation reached 33.8%, with the seven-month accumulated figure at 19.3% and core inflation ticking up to 1.8%. The rebound was driven by seasonal winter-holiday effects — recreation up 5.0%, hotels and restaurants 2.8% — plus a 7.4% jump in fresh vegetables and the lagged pass-through of the peso's roughly 6% depreciation between May and July. Regulated prices (gas, water, transport, electricity) also rose. Analysts broadly call it a "pause," not a reversal: the BCRA's REM projects 1.8% for August, J.P. Morgan sees 2026 closing near 29.5%, while Goldman Sachs lifted its forecast to 30.5%. Caputo insists inflation will converge to international levels "in time."

  • BCRA Slashes Dollar Purchases to 2026 Low to Shield Inflation Fight

    high

    The BCRA cut its foreign-exchange purchases to roughly US$28 million per day in the first half of August, down from US$137 million daily in May — the lowest pace of 2026. The pullback is deliberate: buying fewer dollars means injecting fewer pesos, avoiding fresh fuel for inflation after July's 2.1% rebound. The wholesale peso has been kept near $1,487–$1,500, just under the psychological $1,500 barrier and well inside the crawling band whose ceiling approaches $1,865. Analysts describe a delicate trade-off between containing the dollar and avoiding liquidity shortages that would pressure peso interest rates. The strategy signals the BCRA is now prioritizing disinflation over reserve accumulation, even as gross reserves hover near US$49.5 billion after valuation losses.

  • BCRA Formally Adopts Monetary-Aggregates Regime in Q2 Policy Report

    medium

    In its Q2 Monetary Policy Report (IPOM) published August 6, the BCRA confirmed it has adopted a "monetary aggregates control regime" as the economy's nominal anchor, while making the foreign-exchange and interest-rate markets more flexible. Under the framework — launched with "Stage 4" of the stabilization program — the central bank lets the money supply track the recovery in real money demand, prioritizing reserve accumulation through foreign-currency purchases rather than steering a single reference rate. Short-term peso rates compressed from 35% to 22% effective annual during the first half, though the whole curve rose in August. The shift marks a structural break from inflation targeting: the BCRA now anchors expectations through the quantity of pesos rather than a policy rate.

🏦 Financial & Credit

Consumer credit kept contracting as record delinquency and sky-high financing costs bit. The gap between lending and deposit rates widened to a 15-year high, and defaults began draining supermarket spending. A City of Buenos Aires refinancing program enrolled nine banks as a rare relief intervention.

  • Personal Loans Contract 8% in Real Terms; Rate Gap Hits 15-Year High

    high

    Consumer credit is shrinking, not growing. Lines tied to consumption — credit cards and personal loans, roughly 45% of the loan book — fell every month of 2026, averaging −1.2% monthly and −8.0% in real terms year-to-date, as banks froze new origination amid record delinquency. The spread between lending and deposit rates reached 45.7 percentage points, far above the 22.6-point historical average and the widest in nearly 15 years. Average personal-loan rates sit near 66% TNA before taxes, while a 30-day fixed-term deposit pays about 20%. The BCRA has said it will not intervene. Economist Marina Dal Poggetto notes high rates contain the dollar but choke the recovery, prompting the government's push into dollar-denominated credit.

  • Consumer Credit Cost Tops 200% CFT as Taxes Stack on Rates

    high

    The all-in cost of a personal loan can exceed 200% annually once taxes and fees are added, even though the headline nominal rate may be around 130%. The gap comes from VAT on interest, provincial turnover (Ingresos Brutos) taxes, stamp duties and mandatory insurance. The problem is most acute in digital wallets, where Mercado Pago's annual effective cost (CFTEA) reaches as high as 1,375.94% for thin-file borrowers, versus 63.13% for comparable loans in Brazil. Ualá tops out at 568.94% and Naranja X at 262.66%, while banks range from roughly 93% to 398%. Only about 2% of Mercado Pago's loans carry the maximum rate, applied to new users with no credit history.

  • Record Delinquency Drains Household Budgets and Hits Supermarket Sales

    medium

    Record household delinquency is now spilling into consumption. With bank portfolio irregularity at 12.7% for households and SMEs in June and non-bank and wallet default rates between 29.6% and 32%, roughly seven million borrowers are diverting income toward debt service rather than spending. Noticias Argentinas reports the pattern is visible in supermarket sales, as families with overdue credit card and personal-loan balances cut discretionary purchases. The burden is concentrated among those aged 18–35, where defaults on non-bank cards reach 60%. The dynamic creates a feedback loop: weaker consumption softens economic activity, which in turn pressures repayment capacity further.

  • Buenos Aires Launches 35% Refinancing Program with Nine Banks

    medium

    The City of Buenos Aires opened a Family and Personal Debt Relief Program in partnership with nine banks — Banco Ciudad, Santander, ICBC, Supervielle, Credicoop, Comafi, BBVA, Galicia and Banco Piano. The scheme refinances delinquent debts at a fixed nominal rate capped at 35% annually, with a minimum 24-month term, and applications close October 13. It targets a delinquency crisis that has kept household and SME default near records. The program marks a rare public-sector intervention in a crisis the national government has declined to address with fiscal support, betting instead that disinflation will restore repayment capacity organically.

  • Mercado Pago Faces Usury Complaint and Boycott Over 1,375% Rates

    high

    Mercado Pago was hit with a usury complaint by former legislator Gabriel Solano (Partido Obrero), who alleges some loans carry an annual effective cost (CFTEA) of 1,375.94% — about 21 times the 63.13% the same company charges in Brazil. Solano filed under Article 175 bis of the Penal Code, arguing the fintech "does extraordinary business with the needs of millions of workers." The group Libres del Sur called a boycott of Mercado Libre, Mercado Pago and Mercado Crédito for August 17 under the slogan "Boycott the usurer Galperin," claiming Milei's government allowed wallets to raise rates without limit.   • Wallets Position for New Labor-Assistance Fund Business

    medium

    The CNV is moving to regulate the Fondos de Asistencia Laboral (FAL), opening a new business line for wallets, and three players already hold an early advantage. Mercado Pago operates Mercado Pago Asset Management, Ualá runs Ualá Fondos S.A.U., and Naranja X — part of Grupo Financiero Galicia — controls Naranja Digital Compañía Financiera, a BCRA-authorized entity. All three are registered as fund managers, giving them a potential route into the business. The draft rules — a floor of ten mutual funds under management and a 50 million UVA net-worth requirement — could favor scaled traditional managers over smaller digital entrants. Unlike payments, winning the FAL market will require regulatory standing, capital and track record, not just user numbers.

But markets punished Argentine assets, lifting sovereign risk to 480 basis points. The government pressed ahead with its IMF-agreed credit plan, renewed the China swap, and projected a robust 2027.

  • YPF Files US$51 Billion LNG Project — Largest Investment in Argentine History

    high

    YPF formally applied to enroll its Argentina LNG project in the RIGI incentive regime, marking the largest private investment ever filed in Argentina at roughly US$51 billion over the project's life. YPF is joined by Italy's Eni and XRG, the international arm of Abu Dhabi's ADNOC. The project will liquefy Vaca Muerta shale gas via two floating LNG units off Río Negro, with capacity for 12 million tonnes a year — expandable to 18 — and about US$29 billion to be spent before a 2031 startup. It projects US$10 billion in annual exports over 20 years, 20,000 construction jobs, and US$15 billion in local supplier purchases. A final investment decision is expected by late 2026.
